# Game design

The design principles every game that takes the core shares, for whoever changes a rule, a feature or how the computer
players play; taken only by projects that are games, and read before such a task. Each game's own principles add to
these, and its manifest fills the slots. A feature here is any move, rule or power a player can choose to use.

- **The computer players never know who is a person** (core archive 87). Nothing in the logic of the *computer
  players*, nor in *what they are shown*, says whether another player is a person, and it holds nothing a player in
  their place could not see or know; they judge every player by behaviour alone. So nobody is played differently for
  being a person, and the game tests everyone alike.
- **No feature only people use** (core archive 87, 88 and 89). Every feature a person can use, the *computer
  players* use too, where it pays and with success at least sometimes: one only people use is one the game does not
  test, and it hands them an edge no rule intended. A feature is not finished until the *measured players* use it so,
  by the numbers of the next point.
- **Using a feature must pay, measured both ways** (core archive 85, 86 and 89). For every feature, the
  *use-against-ignore measurement* plays *measured players* that use it against ones that do not, the rule itself
  unchanged, and *where the numbers go* shows how often they used it, how often it worked and whether its users came
  out ahead. If they did not, the change says which it is: the feature does not matter, or they play it badly, which
  is fixed in the same change.
- **No rule untaught** (core archive 84 and 90). Every rule a player needs is in *where rules are taught*, added or
  changed in the same change as the rule, so no player loses to a rule the game never told it.
- **Strong computer players** (core archive 82, 83 and 90). The *computer players* play as competently as they can,
  within the limits their game sets them, never weaker on purpose (a game made harder or easier is made so by its
  setup, never by weakening them): the rules are measured through their play, and weak play hides what is wrong with a
  rule.
- **Don't prohibit; give it its real cost** (core archive 91 and 92), so the player keeps his agency. An action a player
  could realistically take is never forbidden because it seems unwise; the game's own rules make it as costly, risky and
  rare as it really was, so the rare exception stays possible, as Hannibal crossed the Alps. A ban takes a choice away;
  a real cost shows why it was rarely made.
- **No solutions, only trade-offs** (core archive 91 and 93). Every feature helps in some situations and costs in
  others: none is always right or always wrong to use, and the *use-against-ignore measurement* checks that neither
  always using it nor never using it wins.
