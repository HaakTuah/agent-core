# Game design

The design principles every game that takes the core shares, for whoever changes a rule, a feature or how the computer
players play; taken only by projects that are games, and read before such a task. Each game's own principles add to
these. A feature here is any move, rule or power a player can choose to use.

- **The computer players never know who is a person** (core archive 87). Nothing in the logic of the *computer players*,
  nor in *what they are shown*, says whether another player is a person, and it holds nothing a player in their place
  could not see or know; they judge every player by behaviour alone, so the game tests everyone alike.
- **No feature only people use** (core archive 87, 88 and 89). Every feature a person can use, the *computer players*
  use too, where it pays and with success at least sometimes: one only people use is one the game does not test, and it
  hands them an edge no rule intended. A feature is not finished until the *measured players* use it so, by the numbers
  of the next point.
- **Using a feature must pay, measured both ways** (core archive 85, 86 and 89). For every feature, the
  *use-against-ignore measurement* plays *measured players* that use it against ones that do not, the rule itself
  unchanged, and *where the numbers go* shows how often they used it, how often it worked and whether its users came out
  ahead. If they did not, the change says which it is: the feature does not matter, or they play it badly, which is
  fixed in the same change.
- **No rule untaught** (core archive 84 and 90). Every rule a player needs is in *where rules are taught*, added or
  changed in the same change as the rule, so no player loses to a rule the game never told it.
- **Strong computer players** (core archive 82, 83 and 90). The *computer players* play as competently as they can,
  within the limits their game sets them, never weaker on purpose (a game is made easier or harder by its setup
  instead): the rules are measured through their play, and weak play hides what is wrong with a rule.
- **Don't prohibit; give it its real cost** (core archive 91 and 92), so the player keeps his agency. An action a player
  could realistically take is never forbidden because it seems unwise; the game's own rules make it as costly, risky and
  rare as it really was, so the rare exception stays possible, as Hannibal crossed the Alps.
- **No solutions, only trade-offs** (core archive 91 and 93). Every feature helps in some situations and costs in
  others: none is always right or always wrong to use, and the *use-against-ignore measurement* checks that neither
  always using it nor never using it wins.
- **Reality first** (core archive 102 and 104). Where the real world, or the history a game is set in, is interesting
  enough on its own, the game takes it as it was and invents nothing in its place; invention fills only what reality
  leaves silent, or what play cannot bear, and only after trying to keep both.
- **Effects from causes, as deep as pays** (core archive 102 to 104). Every effect comes from a cause, and the deeper and
  better grounded the cause, the more compelling the outcome, even where a player never sees every link: a quantity
  follows what drives it, never a flat share; what goes wrong for a player comes from a cause it could reason about,
  chance standing in only for causes too small to model; nothing appears or vanishes without a cause it could name.
  Depth stops where more simulation no longer pays for what it costs to build and run; a cause hard to convey is a
  reason to teach it better, never to make it shallower.
- **Different data, same rules** (core archive 105). No rule checks which player, faction or character it is: what sets
  one apart lives in its data and starting conditions, which every rule reads alike, never in a rule that names it;
  only presentation and voice may name one.
- **The order of resolution never decides** (core archive 105). When two act at the same moment, the outcome does not
  depend on which the game settles first: they share, or a seeded draw decides.
- **Everything new belongs** (core archive 105). Every new feature feels balanced, cohesive and purposeful, and fits
  the rest of the game.
- **Code decides facts; models phrase them,** in a game with language-model players or characters (core archive 105).
  The game's code decides what is true and what each seat learns; a model adds only voice, personality and judgement
  within its own seat.
